# admin-high_performance_computing

History of HPC Usage and Education in HAAG
This document outlines the history of High-Performance Computing (HPC) resource usage, infrastructure decisions, and education efforts within HAAG. It covers the primary computing resource (PACE ICE), the evaluation of alternative resources, and the professional networks explored to build organizational HPC knowledge.
Primary Resource: PACE ICE Cluster
HAAG's main computing resource is the PACE ICE (Partnership for an Advanced Computing Environment — Instructional Cluster) at Georgia Tech. The instructional cluster is provided free of charge to courses, and researchers are granted access at the start of each semester through a form submission process managed by HAAG administration.
A basic onboarding document for PACE ICE has been created to help researchers get started on the platform. However, the procedure for submitting the access request form each semester is not yet formally documented, and no automated system exists for this process.
https://pace.gatech.edu/
Resource Decision: Instructional vs. Research Cluster (May 2024)
In May 2024, HAAG leadership held discussions with PACE staff to evaluate the best computing option for the organization. The two options considered were the PACE ICE instructional cluster and the PACE research cluster.
The research cluster was ruled out for several reasons: it requires setup on a per-faculty basis, GPU and CPU resources must either be funded by faculty or accessed through a lower-priority backup queue, and resources would be segmented by faculty group rather than shared collectively across HAAG. Asking faculty to fund computing resources was also considered potentially unfeasible.
The instructional cluster was selected because all HAAG researchers share the same partition, GPU and CPU access is free, and access is automatically tied to course enrollment. The PACE ICE cluster has served as HAAG's primary computing environment since this decision.
Known Limitations of PACE ICE
Despite its advantages, the instructional cluster has surfaced several recurring challenges over the course of HAAG's semesters.
Access loss during semester breaks: Because PACE ICE is an instructional resource, researchers regularly lose access during semester transitions. Access must be re-requested each semester, creating gaps in availability.
External collaborator access: Getting Comp Advisors and other external collaborators onto the PACE infrastructure has been difficult. No formal system exists for granting external access. One-off requests may be possible but are not guaranteed, meaning Comp Advisors generally cannot access the environment where researchers are completing their work. This limits Comp Advisors' ability to meaningfully oversee research activity.
Graduating and volunteer researchers: Researchers who graduate or transition to a volunteer role lose their standard access. A separate ticket process exists to restore access for internal volunteers, and this has generally been feasible, but it requires submitting a new request with all volunteer names each semester. This process is not currently documented.
Storage management: HAAG has had to request additional storage from PACE, but no system exists for auditing which projects are actively using storage versus which are dormant. A procedure is needed to regularly assess active and inactive projects and ensure storage usage remains within reasonable bounds.
Exploration of External Computing Resources
To address the limitations of PACE ICE — particularly the lack of a persistent, semester-independent allocation and the access challenges for Comp Advisors — HAAG explored two external computing resources.
NAIRR (National AI Research Resource): A pilot program specifically focused on AI projects. Requires a formal application that assesses the applicant's understanding of their own resource needs. HAAG explored this as a potential supplemental or backup resource.
https://nairrpilot.org/
ACCESS: A general-purpose national computing resource. Also requires an application with similar resource needs assessment. Like NAIRR, ACCESS was considered as a persistent allocation that could operate independently of semester cycles and potentially allow Comp Advisor access.
https://access-ci.org/
A survey was initiated to gather project-level resource requirements — including GPU and storage needs — across HAAG projects. This effort was intended to support both the external resource applications and to build internal understanding of what different projects actually require computationally. This survey work remains ongoing and not yet fully established.
HPC Education and Professional Networks
Alongside resource access, HAAG has explored several professional networks and organizations focused on HPC education, with the goal of increasing organizational HPC literacy — particularly among Comp Advisors and faculty.
Campus Champions: A national network in which one or a few individuals at each university serve as designated points of contact for HPC questions. Identifying and engaging the Campus Champion(s) at Georgia Tech, as well as at affiliated institutions, is one avenue for getting HPC guidance.
https://campuschampions.cyberinfrastructure.org/
CCM-Net (Computing and Communications for Minority-Serving Institutions Network): HAAG explored CCM-Net as a venue for recruiting HPC mentors. Efforts to attract a mentor through the platform were not successful. However, participation in the CCM-Net monthly meetings proved more valuable — the meetings provided opportunities to meet professionals experienced with ACCESS and NAIRR applications, and to build relationships that could support future resource proposals. This remains the most productive use of CCM-Net engagement for HAAG.
https://ccmnet.org/mentorships
CARC (Cyberinfrastructure and Advanced Research Computing): CARC hosts a range of working groups organized by research area and domain. HAAG members have applied to create new CARC groups related to project management, though none of those efforts moved forward. Nonetheless, CARC meetings and working groups — particularly those focused on AI facilitation and Anaconda environments — have been identified as potentially useful for connecting with HPC professionals whose experience overlaps with HAAG's needs.
https://carcc.org/
The Case for HPC Education Within HAAG
A recurring theme across all of these efforts is that HAAG as an organization would benefit from deeper internal HPC knowledge. Better understanding of project-level computing requirements — including GPU needs, storage demands, and expected run times — would benefit HAAG in several ways.
Comp Advisors with stronger HPC literacy would be better equipped to guide researchers on what computations are feasible, how to structure workflows, and what to expect in terms of compute time. Faculty affiliates, who may have limited HPC experience themselves, could receive more informed guidance from HAAG on whether their proposed projects are realistic given available resources. And the organization as a whole would be better positioned to write competitive applications for external resources like NAIRR and ACCESS.
One model under consideration is to train a small number of HAAG members to serve as internal HPC experts, available as a resource across the organization. This approach mirrors the Campus Champions model and could help systematically raise the HPC literacy baseline across research teams.
Undocumented Procedures and Outstanding Gaps
Several processes related to HPC resource management within HAAG remain undocumented or unestablished as of the time of this writing. These include: the semester-by-semester PACE ICE access request procedure; the process for requesting volunteer access to PACE ICE; a system for auditing and managing storage usage on PACE; a formal process for assessing project-level resource requirements; and a procedure for applying to and onboarding onto external resources such as NAIRR and ACCESS. Formalizing these procedures is a priority for the next phase of HAAG's HPC infrastructure development.
